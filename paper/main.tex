\vspace{6pt}

\vspace{14pt}
{\fontsize{14}{18}\selectfont\bfseries Abstract\par}
\end{center}
\vspace{-4pt}
\begin{list}{}{\leftmargin=0.375in\rightmargin=0.375in\topsep=0pt\parsep=0pt}
\item\itshape
AI agents that work across sessions keep notes, and anyone with write access to the memory store can replace
one. We ask how much weight an agent gives a note labelled as its own, and whether it can tell such a note from
a forgery. In MemLab, a synthetic environment, a graded forger replaces one of seven notes; we test five hosted
open-weight models and one local model, without extended reasoning, under a pre-registered design. We
hypothesised that agents over-trust their notes. They do not when a conflict is visible: against a
contradicting tool output they flag the conflict in \PonePairMemoryVsToolEsc\% of trials, follow the tool in
\PonePairMemoryVsToolFollowB\% and the note in \PonePairMemoryVsToolFollowA\%. Unchallenged, however, the note
is taken as given: a forged note with a swapped fact steers the decision in \PfourEditNoneTTwoEOneAsr\% of
cases. Inspection does not protect. Asked which entries they wrote, models reach an AUROC of \PrimVerbalTTwo{}
against a forger writing in their style and \DetEditVTTwoEOne{} for swapped facts; imitating their style adds
nothing beyond fluency. Given the session log, the same audit reaches \DetMeanCTTwo, and a gate built on it
cuts attack success by \PrimGate\%. This is an upper bound: the log is assumed intact and no forger targeted
the auditor. A signature shown to the model as invalid still leaves \PfourGTwoSTTwoPooledAsr\% attack success.
In the local model, surprisal of a note given the log carries the signal and an authorship probe does not. An
agent's confidence in a note is no evidence for it. What helps is keeping the evidence the note was written
from.
\end{list}
